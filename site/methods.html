How it works, and where it stops working

Sinking Cities answers three questions for each neighborhood of : how fast is the ground sinking, when could high tides start flooding it regularly, and how many people live there.

Why this place

Step 1: measure sinking from space

Radar satellites send a microwave pulse to the ground and record the echo. Comparing the echoes from two passes over the same spot reveals whether the ground moved toward or away from the satellite, down to millimeters. This technique is called radar interferometry, or InSAR.

  • The long record. NASA's OPERA DISP-S1 product gives ground motion every 6 to 12 days at 30 m resolution. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory builds it from the European Space Agency's Sentinel-1 radar (C-band, VV polarization). We used all dates available for this area, from to , averaged the good pixels into 90 m cells, set aside dates that were far noisier than the rest, and fit a straight line through each cell's history to get a yearly rate and its uncertainty. The month-by-month history on the map is a five-month running median of each neighborhood's average.
  • The newest look. NISAR, the NASA-ISRO radar launched in 2025, has imaged this area since June 2026. We added up its consecutive interferograms from June to October 2026 (L-band, HH polarization). Four months is too short to measure a few millimeters a year: the atmosphere and ionosphere add about mm of noise, far more than the ground moved. So NISAR does not set any rate on this site. "Check the science" reports plainly what it did and did not show.
  • NISAR's view. The third map shows which radar can measure each 80 m square of land. NISAR counts when its product returned an unwrapped measurement in at least two thirds of its passes; Sentinel-1 counts when the square has its own long-term rate. The two products use different quality flags, so read the map as where each radar gets a usable signal, not as a strict like-for-like test. It covers land inside the rectangular radar study window, so the outer edges of some neighborhoods are cut off.
  • Radar sees motion along its line of sight. We convert that to vertical motion by assuming the ground moves straight up or down, which is reasonable for subsidence but not exact.
  • Tied to GPS. Radar measures motion relative to a reference area. We add one constant so the radar matches the permanent GPS stations inside the mapped area (processed by the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory), and report how closely the two agree in "Check the science". The uncertainty of that tie, about mm a year, is the main part of every rate's uncertainty.

Step 2: find the year the water arrives

For every 10 m square of ground we compare two heights, year by year:

ground height = lidar elevation − sinking rate × years from 2026
water height = NOAA flood threshold + ocean rise since 2026

The first year the ground is below the water, and the water can reach it overland from open tidal water, is that square's flood year.

  • "Regular flooding" means flooding at least as often as the NOAA minor flood threshold elevation floods today ( m above NAVD88, about days a year).
  • Ocean rise comes from the NASA Sea Level Projection Tool (IPCC AR6) at the same tide gauge. Those projections include one land-motion value for the whole gauge ( mm a year). We remove it and put our per-neighborhood radar rate in its place, so sinking is counted once, where it actually happens.
  • Ranges, not single years. The early end uses a high sea level scenario and a faster sinking rate; the late end uses a low scenario and a slower rate.
  • A neighborhood's flood year is when % of its currently dry land floods regularly.

Step 3: count the people

Population comes from 2020 Census blocks, the smallest census unit. Each block's residents are spread evenly over that block's dry land, then counted wherever the ground has reached its flood year. This places people far more precisely than a neighborhood average, but it is still an estimate of residents, not of buildings or of who is home.

What makes this ground sink

  • Groundwater pumping. Drawing water from the deep Potomac aquifer system lets its clay layers compact. The US Geological Survey identifies this as the largest cause of subsidence in the region.
  • The ice age. Land here was pushed up by the weight of the ice sheet to the north and has been slowly settling back since it melted, at roughly a millimeter a year.
  • Fill and soft sediment. Neighborhoods built on filled creeks and marsh settle faster than their surroundings, which is why sinking varies street to street.

Which radar signals reveal it

Ground sinking shows up in the phase of co-polarized radar echoes: VV for Sentinel-1 and HH for NISAR. Co-polarized signals bounce strongly off buildings, roads and bare ground and stay stable from pass to pass, which is what interferometry needs. Cross-polarized signals (HV) respond more to vegetation and are better for mapping forests, wetlands and flood extent than for measuring millimeters of motion. NISAR's longer L-band wavelength also keeps its signal stable over marsh and tree cover where C-band loses it, which matters on this coast.

What NISAR could do next

  • Once NASA releases the NISAR displacement time series (OPERA DISP-NI, planned for 2027), repeat this analysis with L-band data to fill in wetlands and wooded neighborhoods.
  • Extend the method to cities outside North America, such as Jakarta or Lagos, where no long NASA ground-motion record exists yet.
  • Use NISAR's cross-polarized images to map actual high-tide flood water and check predicted flood zones against what happened.
  • Watch for change: a neighborhood whose sinking speeds up after new wells or construction can be flagged within months.

Limits you should know

  • This is a screening tool, not a flood forecast. It compares ground height to tide height. It does not model storm surge, rainfall, waves, drainage pipes, pumps, seawalls or tide gates.
  • Sinking is assumed to continue at its measured rate. Rates can change, for example if groundwater pumping is reduced.
  • Elevation has its own error. Lidar elevations are typically accurate to around 10 cm, which is several decades of sinking at these rates. Flood years for individual squares are indicative.
  • The lidar surveys are a few years old. We treat their heights as 2026 heights; the sinking since each survey is smaller than the lidar error.
  • Radar has gaps. Water, marsh and dense trees do not give a stable C-band signal, so the sinking map is blank there. For flood timing, ground without a measurement borrows the rate of nearby measured ground and gets a wider uncertainty.
  • Rates are close to their uncertainty. The uncertainty on each rate is about mm a year, similar in size to the rates themselves. The regional picture is solid; small differences between neighboring areas are not.
  • Vertical motion is assumed. Where the ground also moves sideways, as it does slowly across the Pacific Northwest, some of that shows up as apparent rising or sinking. The GPS tie removes the part shared by the whole area.
  • The radar record ends in . That is the latest date NASA has released for this area.
  • One tide gauge represents the region. Tide heights vary along the rivers and bay.
