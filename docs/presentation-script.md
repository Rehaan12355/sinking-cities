# Sinking Cities: two-minute script

Seven slides, about 320 words, roughly two minutes at a steady pace. The same
text is in each slide's speaker notes. Times are targets, not measured.

Deck: https://claude.ai/artifact/DWgeB6CeryToN7y2eF6ktr

## 1. Sinking Cities (0:00 to 0:12)

We're Sinking Cities. Our app uses NASA radar to show which parts of a coastal
city are quietly sinking, when they could start flooding regularly, and how many
people live there.

## 2. Two things bring the tide to the street (0:12 to 0:27)

Coastal flooding has two causes. The sea is rising, and in many cities the
ground is also sinking. In Hampton Roads, Virginia, high tides already pass the
flood line about sixteen days a year. We measured both causes, neighborhood by
neighborhood.

## 3. One map, one slider, every neighborhood (0:27 to 0:47)

This is the app. Pick a city and drag the year. Blue is ground that floods
regularly at high tide. Red neighborhoods have a tenth of their land flooding.
Click any neighborhood for its sinking rate, the range of years flooding could
begin, and how many people live there.

## 4. Sinking adds thousands of people to the flood zone (0:47 to 1:09)

Here is what we found. In Hampton Roads, about eighteen thousand people already
live on land that floods regularly. By 2100, sea level rise alone makes that
forty-two thousand. Add the sinking we measured and it is nearly fifty-five
thousand. Charleston tells the same story. Seattle, our home city, is mostly
safe, and we show that honestly.

## 5. NISAR sees the ground the older radar can't (1:09 to 1:34)

The challenge is about NISAR, NASA's newest radar. It has only five months of
data, too short to measure millimeters a year, and we say so. But it already
shows something the older radar can't. Gold is land only NISAR can measure: a
third of Charleston, mostly marsh edges and woods where flooding starts. In a
year, that becomes sinking rates for those blank areas.

## 6. Why you can trust the numbers (1:34 to 1:49)

Can you trust it? We checked the radar against GPS stations, and they agree to
about one and a half millimeters a year. Every flood date is a range, not a
single year, and every number traces to public data.

## 7. Protect people before the water arrives (1:49 to 2:04)

City planners can use this today to see which neighborhoods to protect first.
Next, NISAR lets us do the same for cities outside North America, where far more
people are at risk. The site is live with no login. Thank you.

## If you run long

Cut the Seattle sentence on slide 4 and the last sentence on slide 5. That saves
about ten seconds.

## Likely questions

- **Why isn't NISAR used for the rates?** Five months is too short. Over
  Charleston its readings scatter by about 14 mm while the ground moved under
  1 mm. The rates come from NASA's OPERA product, nine years of radar.
- **How accurate are the rates?** In Hampton Roads they agree with nine GPS
  stations to about 1.4 mm a year. Charleston and Seattle have only four
  stations each inside the map, so their uncertainty rests on fewer checks.
- **Is this a flood forecast?** No. It compares ground height with tide height.
  It does not model storms, rain, drains or sea walls.
- **Why does Seattle show so little?** Its shoreline is steep and its low ground
  is mostly port and industrial land, so few residents are exposed. Rare extreme
  tides, like December 2022, are above the "regular flooding" level we map.
